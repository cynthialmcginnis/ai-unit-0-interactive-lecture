1. You Already Use AI Every Day

Think About Navigation Apps

Open a navigation app, type a destination, and a route appears in seconds. It accounts for traffic, road closures, and sometimes the way you usually drive. That app is AI, and it runs several kinds at once.

What's happening under the hood:
  • Collecting speed reports from thousands of phones on the road
  • Turning that data into a live traffic picture
  • Running a search algorithm comparing possible routes
  • Picking the fastest route based on predictions
  • Learning your habits to suggest your usual route

You already know how this app behaves. You've watched it reroute you around a crash, and you've seen it get the arrival time wrong. This unit gives you the vocabulary to explain what's happening underneath.

Keep these three questions in mind for every system you meet:
  1. What patterns does it recognize?
  2. What did it learn from?
  3. What decision does it make with that?

2. Key Terms

Understanding the Hierarchy

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a computer system built to do tasks that normally take human intelligence. Those tasks include solving problems, recognizing speech, interpreting images, making decisions, and translating languages.

Three terms nest inside each other, and people mix them up constantly. Understanding the difference is crucial:

Artificial Intelligence (AI)
The whole field. Any system that does work we associate with human thinking.

Term What it means Example
Artificial Intelligence The whole field. Any system that does work we associate with human thinking. A rule-based tax program, a spam filter, a chatbot
Machine Learning (ML) A subset of AI. The system learns patterns from data instead of following rules a person wrote for every case. A music app that gets better at picking songs as you listen
Deep Learning A subset of ML. It uses neural networks with many layers to find complex patterns. Face unlock on your phone, speech-to-text
Key insight: Every deep learning system is machine learning, and every machine learning system is AI. The reverse isn't true. A system that follows hand-written rules is AI but isn't machine learning.

Three Working Terms You'll Use Constantly

Term What it means
Algorithm The set of steps the system follows to learn from data or to make a decision.
Training Data The examples the system learns from.
Model What the system ends up with after training. It's the learned pattern the system uses on new cases.

Example: How a spam filter works

  • Training data: Thousands of emails already marked spam or not spam
  • Algorithm: The system works through those examples and finds patterns—certain phrases, strange links, unknown senders
  • Model: The result. When a new email arrives, the model scores it and decides where it goes

3. Major Categories of AI Systems

AI isn't one technology. You can sort any AI system three ways: by how it reasons, by what it does, and by how it's built. A system can fall into several categories at once.

By How It Reasons

Symbolic AI (rule-based or classical AI) follows rules that people write. An early medical expert system might say: "If the patient has a fever and a stiff neck, consider meningitis." Rules like that are easy to check, but they break the moment a case comes up that nobody wrote a rule for.

Machine Learning learns its rules from data. It comes in three main types:

Type How it learns Example
Supervised Learning From labeled examples, where each example comes with the right answer A spam filter trained on emails already marked spam or not spam
Unsupervised Learning From unlabeled data. It finds groups and patterns no one pointed out. A retailer sorting customers into groups it never defined in advance
Reinforcement Learning From trial and error. It gets rewards for good actions and penalties for bad ones. A program that learns to win a game by playing millions of rounds
The loan approval dilemma: A symbolic system approves a loan because a person wrote the rule, and you can read that rule. A machine learning system approves it because patterns in past loans suggest it, and often no one can point to the exact reason. Which is more trustworthy?

By What It Does

Category What it works with Examples
Natural Language Processing (NLP) Human language Translation apps, virtual assistants, chatbots, sentiment analysis
Computer Vision Images and video Face unlock, medical image screening, damage detection in aircraft inspection photos
Robotics The physical world Warehouse robots, surgical assistants, bomb disposal robots
Recommendation Systems Your past choices Streaming suggestions, online shopping, social media feeds

By How It's Built

  • Neural Networks: Layers of connected nodes, loosely modeled on neurons in the brain. They power most modern AI, including deep learning.
  • Decision Trees: Reach an answer through a chain of yes-or-no questions, so you can trace exactly how they decided.
  • Genetic Algorithms: Borrow from natural selection. They generate many possible solutions, keep the best, and combine them, round after round. They work well on hard scheduling and design problems.

Real systems often mix approaches. A self-driving car uses computer vision to see the road, machine learning to predict what other drivers will do, and hand-written rules for things that must never change, like stopping at a red light.

4. A Short History of AI

AI's history runs in cycles. A breakthrough creates excitement, promises outrun results, funding dries up, and then a new approach brings the field back. Knowing that pattern helps you judge today's claims.

1950
1950
The Turing Test
Alan Turing proposes a test: can a machine hold a conversation you can't tell apart from a person's? This is the first serious attempt to define machine intelligence.
55-56
1955–1956
Birth of AI
John McCarthy and colleagues propose a summer workshop at Dartmouth, coining the term "artificial intelligence." The field gets its name and its founding goal.
50-60
1950s–1960s
Early Wins
Arthur Samuel's checkers program learns from experience. The Logic Theorist proves math theorems. ELIZA imitates a therapist in conversation. Early wins spark predictions that human-level AI is only years away.
❄️
1974–1980
First AI Winter
Programs fail on language and common sense, and government funding (including DARPA's) is cut back. Hype meets the limits of the era's computers and methods.
80s
1980s
Expert Systems Boom
Expert systems capture specialists' knowledge in rules and sell well. Japan launches its Fifth Generation computing project in 1982. AI becomes a business for the first time.
❄️
1987–1993
Second AI Winter
Expert systems prove costly to maintain, hard to update, and unable to learn. Hand-written rules hit a ceiling.
1997
1997
Deep Blue Defeats Kasparov
IBM's Deep Blue defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov. A narrow system beats the best human at one task.
2012
2012
Deep Learning Revolution
A neural network called AlexNet wins an image recognition contest by a wide margin. Deep learning takes over, powered by GPUs and huge datasets.
2016
2016
AlphaGo Beats Lee Sedol
DeepMind's AlphaGo beats Go champion Lee Sedol in Seoul. A milestone many experts expected to be a decade away.
2017
2017
The Transformer
Google researchers publish the transformer design. This becomes the foundation of today's large language models.
2022
2022
ChatGPT Goes Public
OpenAI releases ChatGPT. GenAI reaches the public, and millions use it within weeks.

What Changed?

Between the AI winters and today's boom, one crucial shift happened:

  • Early AI: Tried to write intelligence down as rules
  • Modern AI: Learns from data

That shift only worked once computers got fast enough and data got big enough.

5. Narrow AI and General AI

Narrow AI (Artificial Narrow Intelligence)

Every AI system in use today is narrow AI. Artificial narrow intelligence (ANI), also called weak AI, is built for one task or a small set of related tasks. It can be excellent inside that boundary and useless outside it.

Example: The system that reads chest X-rays can't schedule your appointment.

You use narrow AI constantly:

  • Face unlock
  • Navigation
  • Spam filters
  • Translation apps
  • Voice assistants
  • Fraud detection on your bank card

General AI (Artificial General Intelligence)

Artificial general intelligence (AGI), also called strong AI, would learn and apply knowledge across any field the way a person does. It could take on any intellectual task a human can. No such system exists. Researchers disagree sharply about whether one will, and when.

The GenAI Complication

GenAI chatbots complicate the picture. One chatbot can write an email, explain a tax form, and debug code, which looks general. It still fails in ways a person wouldn't:
  • It invents sources
  • It loses track of simple facts
  • It can't check its own work against the real world

Breadth isn't the same as general intelligence.

Why This Distinction Matters

When you read the news about AI risk, articles often slide between today's narrow systems and a future AGI without saying so. Always ask: which one does the writer mean?

6. AI Technologies Today

The technologies from earlier sections have moved fast in the past decade. Three things drove it: faster hardware, better algorithms, and far more data.

Technology Where it stands now
Large Language Models (LLMs) The engines behind GenAI chatbots. They learn from huge amounts of text and generate new text one piece at a time.
Natural Language Processing Has moved past keyword matching to handling tone, context, and meaning. Powers assistants, customer service bots, and content moderation.
Computer Vision Goes well beyond labeling a photo. Reads medical scans, inspects parts on assembly lines, and guides drones and vehicles.
Reinforcement Learning Trains systems through rewards. A version called reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is used to make chatbots more helpful and less harmful.
Generative AI (GenAI) Creates new content instead of only analyzing it. Diffusion models and generative adversarial networks (GANs) produce realistic images from a text description.
Autonomous Systems Sense, decide, and act in the physical world: self-driving cars, delivery robots, and uncrewed aircraft.
Important: Each of these tools has limits, and knowing them matters as much as knowing what they can do. You'll test those limits yourself in the next unit.
